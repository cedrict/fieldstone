\section*{Background}

This \stone is inspired/based on the following articles:
\begin{itemize}
\item \fullcite{jeff21} (2d only):

\begin{displayquote}
``In this paper the complete solution is given for bipolar co-ordinates, for which the
co-ordinate curves are co-axial circles. This solution enables us to treat the problems
of an infinite plate containing two circular holes, a semi-infinite plate bounded by a
straight edge and containing one circular hole, and a circular disc with an eccentric
circular hole.''
\end{displayquote}
 
\item \fullcite{mcti87} (3d only) :
\begin{displayquote}
``Approximate solutions are obtained for the stress and displacement fields due to a 
pressurized spherical cavity in an elastic half-space.''
\end{displayquote}


\item \fullcite{vebo96} (2d only):
\begin{displayquote}
``An approximate analytical solution is presented,
considering the soil as a linear elastic material.
Although it is realized that this is a very poor
schematization of the real behaviour of soils, an
elastic solution may well serve to investigate some
of the characteristics of the resulting fields of stress
and strain, and it may also serve as a reference for
more refined (numerical) computations.
[...]
Here the surface displacements for a
tunnel in a semi-infinite medium are considered, as
well as the displacements and stresses throughout
the half space.
[...]
In these solutions the displacements are of order ${\cal O}(1/r)$, so that they correspond
to the removal of a finite volume, and the stresses are of order ${\cal O}(1/r^2)$.''
\end{displayquote}

\item \fullcite{verr98} (2d only):
\begin{displayquote}
``An analytical solution is given of the class of problems of an elastic half plane with a
circular cavity, loaded on the cavity boundary.''
\end{displayquote}


\end{itemize}
All publications deal with a 2d/3d cavity in an elastic medium and present 
a (semi-)analytical solution.

Note that my python code is only 2d but given the axisymmetric nature 
of 3d setups I could easily modify it to allow for an 
axisymmetric formulation later on.

\section*{Scrutinizing Verruijt \& Booker 1996}

The paper gives an approximate analytic solution for a tunnel in a homogeneous elastic half
space, caused by ground loss and ovalization. The two basic processes are illustrated in
figure 1.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=8cm]{python_codes/fieldstone_186/images/fig1}\\
{\captionfont Ground loss and ovalization of a tunnel. Taken from \cite{vebo96}.}
\end{center}

The surface displacements for a tunnel in a semi-infinite medium are considered, as
well as the displacements and stresses throughout the elastic half space.
In what follows we disregard the ovalization, so $\delta=0$ (see paper).

Note that the pdf of the paper also contains additional material with 
full derivations of all fields. 

Very important remark: at no point in this paper the authors 
specify boundary conditions on the tunnel walls (no displacement nor
stress). They merely arrive at a class of (approximate) solutions that fulfill
the free surface boundary conditions. 
The $\epsilon$ parameter acts in fact as a scaling coefficient for
the amplitude of displacements and stresses.

As such that article is not so useful. The analytical displacement and stresses
are not exact on the tunnel wall so that neither can be 
prescribed there. As it turns out to be, the 1998 paper by Verruit (see here after)
is much better.

\subsection*{Analytical displacement}

The total displacement is the sum of two components. The first is given by
Eqs 1,2 of \textcite{vebo96}:

\begin{eqnarray}
u_x &=& -\epsilon R^2 \left(  \frac{x}{r_1^2}+\frac{x}{r_2^2} \right) \nn\\
u_z &=& -\epsilon R^2 \left(  \frac{z_1}{r_1^2}+\frac{z_2}{r_2^2} \right) \nn
\end{eqnarray}

with the coordinate system defined as follows:
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=4cm]{python_codes/fieldstone_186/images/fig2}\\
{\captionfont  Taken from \cite{vebo96}.}
\end{center}


Also note that $u_z=0$ for $z=0$ since $z_1=-z_2$.
The second component is given by Eqs 10,11 of \textcite{vebo96}:

\begin{eqnarray}
u_x &=& -\frac{2\epsilon R^2 x}{m} \left( \frac{1}{r_2^2} - \frac{2mzz_2}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn\\
u_z &=&  \frac{2\epsilon R^2  }{m} \left( \frac{(m+1)z_2}{r_2^2} - \frac{mz(x^2-z_2^2)}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn
\end{eqnarray}
with $m=(1-2\nu)^{-1}$.

As stated in the paper, the complete solution is the sum of this solution and the previous 
expressions, i.e.:

\begin{eqnarray}
u_x &=& -\epsilon R^2 \left(  \frac{x}{r_1^2}+\frac{x}{r_2^2} \right) 
 -\frac{2\epsilon R^2 x}{m} \left( \frac{1}{r_2^2} - \frac{2mzz_2}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn\\
u_z &=& -\epsilon R^2 \left(  \frac{z_1}{r_1^2}+\frac{z_2}{r_2^2} \right) 
+  \frac{2\epsilon R^2  }{m} \left( \frac{(m+1)z_2}{r_2^2} - \frac{mz(x^2-z_2^2)}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn
\end{eqnarray}
Note that the factor $(m+1)/m$ in the equation above can also
be written as $2(1-\nu)$. 
Then velocity components can be written as follows:
\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{1}{\epsilon R^2} u_x 
&=& -  \left(  \frac{x}{r_1^2}+\frac{x}{r_2^2} \right) 
 - 2 \left( \frac{x (1-2\nu) }{r_2^2} - \frac{2xzz_2}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn\\
\frac{1}{\epsilon R^2} u_z 
&=& - \left(  \frac{z_1}{r_1^2}+\frac{z_2}{r_2^2} \right) 
+  2  \left( \frac{2(1-\nu)z_2}{r_2^2} - \frac{z(x^2-z_2^2)}{r_2^4}   \right) \nn
\end{eqnarray}


In the additional notes at the end of the paper we find\footnote{The 
'basic solution for ground loss' part of the additional notes 
needs to be discarded here. Also, these additional notes use the $y$
coordinate instead of $z$ in the paper so I have changed $y\rightarrow z$
because I will use the $y$ coordinate later.}:

\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{1}{\epsilon R^2} u_x &=&
-\left(\frac{x}{r_1^2}+\frac{x}{r_2^2} \right)
-2 \left(\frac{(1-2\nu)x}{r_2^2} - \frac{2xzz_2}{r_2^4} \right)
\nn\\
\frac{1}{\epsilon R^2} u_z &=&
-\left(\frac{z_1}{r_1^2}+\frac{z_2}{r_2^2} \right)
+2 \left(\frac{2(1-\nu)z_2}{r_2^2} - \frac{z(x^2-z_2^2)}{r_2^4} \right)
\end{eqnarray}
where $z_1=z-h$ and $z_2=z+h$, and which is identical to the expressions
of the first part of the article.

In particular the vertical component of the velocity at the surface is given by Eq 12:
\[
u_z(x,z=0)
= 2 \epsilon R^2 \frac{m+1}{m} \frac{h}{x^2+h^2}
= 4 \epsilon R^2 (1-\nu) \frac{h}{x^2+h^2}
\]

In the code the origin of the Cartesian coordinate system 
is placed at the center of the tunnel, with the $y$-axis 
pointing upward.

In this case we have $z=L-y$, and then 
\begin{eqnarray}
z_1&=&z-h=L-y-L=-y  \\
z_2&=&z+h=L-y+L=-y+2L \\
r_1&=& \sqrt{(x-0)^2+(y-0)^2} = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}   \\
r_2&=& \sqrt{(x-0)^2+(y-2L)^2} = \sqrt{x^2+(y-2L)^2}
\end{eqnarray}

Also, because the vertical axis has been reversed, we then have $u_y=-u_z$.

\subsection*{Analytical stress}

In the original paper only the isotropic stress $\sigma_0=\frac12(\sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy})$
at the surface is presented in Eq. 14:
\[
\sigma_0 = -4 \mu \epsilon R^2 \frac{z_2^2-x^2}{r_2^4}
\]

We need to turn to the additional material to find:

\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{1}{2\mu\epsilon R^2} \sigma_{xx}&=& 
\left( \frac{x^2-y_1^2}{r_1^4}+\frac{x^2-y_2^2}{r_2^4} \right) 
+2 \left( \frac{x^2-y_2^2}{r_2^4}-\frac{2yy_2(3x^2-y_2^2)}{r_2^6}  \right)
\nn\\
\frac{1}{2\mu\epsilon R^2} \sigma_{yy}&=& 
- \left(  \frac{x^2-y_1^2}{r_1^4}+\frac{x^2-y_2^2}{r_2^4}    \right) 
+2\left( \frac{x^2-y_2^2}{r_2^4}+\frac{2yy_2(3x^2-y_2^2)}{r_2^6}  \right)
\nn\\
\frac{1}{2\mu\epsilon R^2} \sigma_{xy} &=& 
 \left( \frac{2xy_1}{r_1^4}+\frac{2xy_2}{r_2^4}  \right)
+2 \frac{2xy(x^2-3y_2^2)}{r_2^6}
\nn
\end{eqnarray}

We also have the following necessary requirements so that the mesh topology is 
acceptable: 
\lstinline{nelyD=nelyC},
\lstinline{nelxD=nelxB},
\lstinline{nelxC=nelxA} and 
\lstinline{nelyB=nelyA=nelxA}
so in the end only \lstinline{nelxA}, \lstinline{nelxB} and \lstinline{nelyC} 
remain, which yields the falling function call in the main script: 

\begin{lstlisting}
x_V,y_V,icon_V,nn_V,nel,Nfem=mesher(nelxA,nelxB,nelyC,L,extent_x,extent_y,radius,m_V)
\end{lstlisting}

Note that \lstinline{extent_x} and \lstinline{extent_y} are calculated as a multiple of $L$:
\begin{lstlisting}
extent_x=xi*L
extent_y=psi*L
nelxB=int(xi*nelxA)
nelyC=int(psi*nelxA)
\end{lstlisting}

\section*{The GTECTON setup/experiment (experiment=1)}

This experiment matches with the \textcite{verr98} (1998) article.
Lukas has provided me with the setup and the 
results obtained with GTECTON for reference (see figures below).

The tunnel has a radius of 2~\si{\kilo\meter} and is situated 7~\si{\kilo\meter} below the surface.
The inside pressure is set to 5~\si{\mega\pascal}.
The Poisson ratio is $\nu=0.35$ and the Young's modulus is $E=5\cdot 10^{10}~\si{\pascal}$.
This yields $\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} \simeq 18.5e9$. 

Boundary conditions are free slip on the left side (except on the tunnel walls), 
free surface at the top and free slip on the bottom and right boundaries.

We find that the domain size matters a lot, and in order to come close to 
the analytical solution\footnote{I have not written a code to 
compute the analytical solution, I use here the files provided by Lukas}, 
we must use large values of $\xi$ and $\psi$.
